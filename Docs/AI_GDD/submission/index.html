Lighting the Storm

Part 1

Executive Summary

A 30-minute first-person atmospheric adventure about keeping a light alive through one storm, built by a solo developer with AI assistance. A 10-minute prototype comes first, over six weeks.

Pitch and player promise

You are the new apprentice to Ted, keeper of a remote, storm-battered lighthouse on Bracken Isle. You work through the building’s failing systems under his direction, but the beacon itself won’t stop flickering. A rowboat is driven onto Bracken Isle by the weak light; you watch it crash from the window, then open the lighthouse gate so the two survivors, Jeff and Daniel, can get inside. With Jeff beside you, you cross the storm in an engine boat to fetch a replacement burner from an abandoned outpost, then race back to fit it before a passenger ship reaches the rocks.

The central player skill is steering a powered boat past rocks and other hazards while the storm worsens. The experience should deliver sustained dread, hands-on mechanical work, and a consequential ending. There is no combat, no monster reveal and no jump scares. Folk-horror details invite interpretation without ever confirming a supernatural cause.

Design pillars

Every feature and story beat is judged against four pillars. Each has a test question used in reviews and cuts.

Pillar 1

The light must return

Everything leads back to the beacon. It is what you repair, it guides you home, and it decides whether the ship lives.

Test: does this connect to getting the light back, or to what happens if it fails?

Pillar 2

The storm sets the clock

Pressure comes from the world: the approaching ship’s lights, worsening weather, falling visibility and rocks in the dark. Never a timer, meter or enemy.

Test: can the player feel time running out without any UI?

Pillar 3

Your hands, your eyes

Everything is first-person and physical. You fix things by hand, watch the crash through the window, and see the ship’s fate from where you stand. Guidance comes from Ted and Jeff, not markers.

Test: is this happening to the player in the world, or being shown to them?

Pillar 4

Dread you can read

Sustained unease and ambiguity, never jump scares or a confirmed monster. The player always knows what to do and why they failed: one steering control, clear hazards, fair and recoverable mistakes.

Test: does this add unease without adding confusion?

Scope rule: small world, read closely. One lighthouse, one stretch of sea, one abandoned outpost, and no new major locations. A feature that fails a pillar’s test, or expands the world without deepening the main loop, is deferred.

Cast

  • The Apprentice (player). Characterised through action and a handwritten objective journal rather than dialogue trees.
  • Ted, the keeper. Late 50s, thirty years in the tower. Gruff and blunt, with a guarded sense of care. He directs the repairs out loud from elsewhere in the building.
  • Jeff, the older seaman. Hard, coarse and unhappy about helping, but his instinct is to keep people alive. He is the player’s companion on both crossings and calls out navigation warnings.
  • Daniel, the younger seaman. Shaken by the crash and just glad to be alive. He stays at the lighthouse.

Stretch goal

Wave hazards that push or roll the boat are a stretch goal for both the prototype and the full game. The crossings are designed to work fully without them. Waves are added only after steering passes its week-2 go/no-go test and only if the schedule has room, and they are the first thing cut if it slips.

Outcome checks

Prototype

  • A new player understands what to repair, where to head, and which hazard to avoid, with no timer UI.
  • The boat has one steering control, one fixed speed, clear feedback for near misses and collisions, and a recoverable error state.
  • The ship’s lights and position, seen in first person, communicate the stakes.
  • Both the win and fail outcomes can be reached.

Full game

  • A first-time playthrough lasts about 30 minutes.
  • The final game preserves unexplained environmental wrongness.
Part 2

Game Mechanics

What the player does, in the order they do it, and the systems that shape each action.

Core loop

The loop mirrors one worsening night on duty and plays once through the main story.

  1. 01
    Repair the lighthouse. Ted barks which system is broken and roughly where its part is. The player explores, diagnoses and fixes it with a fixed toolkit. The beacon alone keeps flickering. No deadline
  2. 02
    Watch the crash, open the gate. Ted calls the player to the window, where a rowboat crashes onto Bracken Isle. He tells the player to open the lighthouse gate; Jeff and Daniel make their own way inside. Ted explains the burner must be fetched from the abandoned outpost. No deadline
  3. 03
    Board the engine boat with Jeff. Boarding starts the hidden passenger-ship deadline, and Jeff spells out how much trouble they are in. Deadline starts
  4. 04
    Cross to the outpost. Steer past rocks, buoys and debris in the storm while Jeff shouts directions.
  5. 05
    Pick up the burner. Find it at the outpost and get it aboard.
  6. 06
    Cross back. The storm peaks: denser hazards, lower visibility, fragile cargo aboard.
  7. 07
    Fit the burner. Climb to the lamp room and install it as the ship’s lights close on the rocks.
  8. 08
    Win or fail. Beacon lit in time: the ship sails calmly into port. Deadline missed: the ship runs aground. Outcome

Lighthouse repairs

The lighthouse runs on interdependent systems: the lamp and optic assembly, the clockwork rotation mechanism, the fuel and fog-signal lines, and stormproofing (shutters and seals). Each broken system is fixed through a short, first-person puzzle, such as rewiring a junction box, swapping a gear or patching a leak, using a small fixed toolkit rather than a crafting menu. The prototype includes one repair; the full game includes the full set.

  • Ted’s guidance is the first assist mechanism. He names the part and roughly where to find it; how specific he is scales with the difficulty preset.
  • The journal is a handwritten-style checklist of what is broken, updated as systems are diagnosed. It reflects progress but never controls it.
  • Gating. Some repairs open new areas, for example repairing the winch opens the hatch to the boat dock. This gives one building light Metroidvania-style gating without a large map.
  • The beacon as inciting incident. Once every other system works, the beacon still flickers. That unresolved fault draws Ted to the window and triggers the crash, so a mechanical dead end becomes the story’s turn instead of a quest marker.

The crash and the gate

The crash is scripted and seen entirely in first person through the lighthouse window. It triggers when the window is in view, with a short fallback so it cannot be missed. Opening the gate is a single interaction; Jeff and Daniel walk in on their own, with no escort or rescue. Opening the gate advances the story and opens the route to the engine boat, where Jeff waits.

Sea crossing and boat navigation

The player pilots a small engine boat at one fixed speed with one control: steering. There is no throttle and no oars. The challenge is steering past placed hazards (rocks, reefs, buoys and floating debris) along a route that narrows and darkens as the storm worsens. The sea surface moves for atmosphere but does not push the boat.

  • Jeff’s calls are the second assist mechanism. His warnings read from the same hazard data as the world, so they never disagree with the real threat. How early and how often he calls scales with the difficulty preset.
  • Navigation aids: a compass, the lighthouse beam as a guiding light when heading home, and silhouettes of buoys and rocks in low visibility.
  • Risk states: a collision takes on water, slows the boat, or causes a brief capsize and recovery at a nearby safe point, all costing time. On the return leg, rough handling can knock the burner loose, costing time to recover it. The burner is never permanently lost (proposed).
  • Stretch goal: authored waves that push and roll the boat, added as an optional layer only if time allows.

Tension and the deadline

The central pressure is a countdown the player never sees as a number. It starts only when the player boards the boat; everything before that plays at its own pace. From then on, the approaching ship’s lights on the horizon and the rising storm (wind volume, rain density, sea swell, visibility) communicate how much time is left. It is the only timer in the game: the same deadline runs through both crossings, the pickup and the final repair.

Most failures are soft. A collision costs time rather than ending the run, which keeps the player in the fiction. The exception is the deadline itself, which is a true fail state, so the ending means something.

Difficulty and accessibility

Difficulty is mainly a pacing and tension curve, not a skill gate. A single preset controls how specific Ted’s directions are, how much Jeff helps on the water, and how intense the hazards are, without changing the story or outcomes. Navigation calls are subtitled with speaker names and an optional visual direction cue. Steering and interaction are remappable, with adjustable sensitivity and a reduced-hazard preset.

Presentation

All viewpoints are first-person, with no cutaway or exterior cameras. Warm oil-lamp light indoors plays against blue-grey storm light outside, and the beacon’s sweeping beam is the constant visual anchor. Sound is diegetic first: wind, rain, groaning timber, machinery, buoy bells and a distant foghorn, with no shock stingers. Strange symbols, unexplained shrines and architecture that doesn’t quite fit suggest a history without ever explaining it.

Part 3

AI Architecture

The game is built by one developer working with a team of AI agents. Each agent owns one department of the build. Below, each is described through what the player experiences as a result of its work.

Scope of AI use

AI is used only during development. The shipped game makes no AI calls at runtime: Ted’s and Jeff’s lines, the storm and the ship are authored and scripted, so the game has no API dependency or per-player running cost.

Principles

  • One agent, one department. Each agent works from a bounded task brief naming its goal, inputs, owned files, interfaces and acceptance checks.
  • The developer owns judgement. Steering feel, tone, art fit, dialogue performance and story interpretation are human decisions. AI output is a first pass that the developer reviews and merges.
  • The pillars bind every agent. Work that fails a pillar’s test is returned, whatever its technical quality.
  • No silent canon. When a story or design choice is unresolved, the agent stops and asks instead of deciding.

The agents and their effect on play

Boat / Physics agent

Serves pillar 4 · the core skill
What the player experiences

The feel of every crossing: how quickly the boat answers the stick, how clearly a near miss or collision registers, and how quickly the player recovers and gets back on course.

What it builds
  • Boat controller at one fixed speed
  • Hazard collisions and recovery
  • Tuning values held as data
  • Stretch: wave layer behind an on/off setting

Review gate: the developer plays the week-2 steering spike and makes the go/no-go call on feel.

Level Layout agent

Serves pillar 3 and the scope rule
What the player experiences

Where they can go and what they can see: the climb through the tower, the window that frames the crash, the gate, a sea route that narrows toward the outpost, and sightlines to the beacon and the ship.

What it builds
  • Greybox lighthouse, sea corridor and outpost
  • Interaction anchors for other agents
  • Sightline checks for the crash and ending

Review gate: traversal and sightline checks in the engine.

Gameplay / Systems agent

Serves pillars 1 and 2
What the player experiences

The rules of the night: the story advancing in the right order, the deadline starting the moment they board, fair time penalties for mistakes, and an ending that resolves once as a win or a fail.

What it builds
  • Phase controller and save state
  • Repair jobs from data
  • Crash trigger, gate and boarding
  • Hidden deadline, cargo, win and fail

Review gate: the 10-minute slice plays end to end with no dead ends.

Lighting agent

Serves pillars 1, 2 and 4
What the player experiences

The beacon as both objective and guide home, warm lamplight against the storm, and darkness that deepens as time runs out while hazards stay readable.

What it builds
  • Beacon sweep and dynamic lights
  • Storm visibility presets by phase
  • Minimum hazard-visibility rules

Review gate: the beacon and hazards stay legible at every phase.

Sound agent

Serves pillars 2 and 4
What the player experiences

A storm they can hear getting worse, which tells them time is short without a timer, and Jeff’s voice cutting through it when they need a warning. No shock stingers.

What it builds
  • Layered wind, rain, machinery and foghorn
  • State-based mix and escalation
  • Source list and licences

Review gate: Jeff’s warnings remain audible at peak storm.

Asset agent

Serves pillars 3 and 4
What the player experiences

The physical objects they handle during repairs, and the unsettling details (symbols, shrines, out-of-date posters) that make the lighthouse feel wrong.

What it builds
  • First-pass props and set dressing
  • Source meshes and import notes
  • Provenance records for every asset

Review gate: assets import cleanly and fit the approved reference sheet.

Dialogue agent

Serves pillars 3 and 4
What the player experiences

Ted’s directions during repairs, Jeff’s calls on the water, and the journal’s objectives. Together these are how the player knows what to do without markers, and they get sparser on harder settings.

What it builds
  • Lines for Ted, Jeff and Daniel as data
  • Subtitle and difficulty variants
  • Journal text

Review gate: the developer approves voice and lore before anything is recorded.

QA agent

Serves all pillars
What the player experiences

A night with no dead ends: every phase finishes, both outcomes can be reached, and mistakes are recoverable.

What it builds
  • Reproducible scenario list
  • Bug reports against acceptance checks
  • Checks with waves on and off

Review gate: the developer reproduces and prioritises each finding.

These are roles, not eight agents running at once. One agent can take successive bounded tasks.

Build sequence

  1. Step 1DeveloperSettles open decisions: engine, Ted’s presence, feel target, hardware.
  2. Step 2Boat / PhysicsBuilds the steering spike. Other content waits until it passes.
  3. Step 3Layout + SystemsGreybox route, anchors, event IDs, running 10-minute slice.
  4. Step 4Lighting + SoundJust enough feedback to judge hazards, storm, beacon and ship.
  5. Step 5Dialogue + AssetApproved content, once the slice proves the route.
  6. Step 6QA + DeveloperFirst-time play, all transitions, both outcomes.

Handoff review

Every handoff returns editable source, licences for any third-party material, test instructions and known limitations, and is checked against the same questions:

  • Does it run in the integrated project from a clean checkout?
  • Are state changes and event IDs consistent with the shared contract?
  • Does it pass its task’s acceptance checks?
  • Does it pass each pillar’s test and the scope rule, without deciding an unresolved story point?
  • What did human playtesting show about feel, clarity and pacing?
Part 4

Technical Strategy

Engine, build contract and schedule; how the agent roles are organised; the token budget; and the API and plan constraints the work runs under.

Engine

Proposed: Godot 4 with GDScript. It is free with no royalties, iterates quickly on modest hardware, and stores scenes as plain text, so AI agents can read and edit scenes as well as scripts. Its main weakness, no built-in ocean, is off the critical path now that waves are a stretch goal.

Unity is the alternative if the developer already knows C#. Unreal is ruled out because Blueprints shut out AI help and iteration is slow. Phaser cannot meet the first-person brief (pillar 3).

Build contract

  • Separately owned scenes: lighthouse, sea, outpost, shared player and interaction scenes, and one central game-state controller.
  • Content as data with stable IDs: repairs, dialogue, assist presets and journal text.
  • Explicit phases: LIGHTHOUSE_REPAIRS → CRASH_AND_GATE → BOARDING → OUTBOUND → OUTPOST → RETURN → FINAL_REPAIR → RESOLUTION (WIN | FAIL)
  • Invariants: the deadline is inactive before boarding; Jeff is the only companion; pickup precedes return; the outcome resolves exactly once.
  • Events, not side effects: audio, UI and dialogue respond to game events and never change progression directly.
  • Shared hazard forecast: the hazards, the boat’s reaction and Jeff’s calls read from one source, so warnings always match the real threat.

API and plan constraints

ConstraintEffect on the project
Development-time onlyNo runtime AI in the shipped game, so no API keys, latency, per-player cost or availability risk after release.
Plan usage limitsDevelopment runs on a Claude Team plan with rolling 5-hour and weekly limits. Extra usage is off, so hitting a limit pauses work until it resets. The prototype pace is estimated at about 30–50% of the weekly limit; this is checked at the end of week 1.
Context windowsOpus 5.5 and Sonnet 5 accept up to 1M tokens; Haiku 4.5 accepts 200K. Narrow task briefs keep contexts far below this, which also keeps cost down.

Cost leversRoute routine work to Sonnet 5 or Haiku 4.5, keep contexts small, and start fresh sessions between unrelated tasks.
Asset rightsEvery AI-generated or third-party asset carries a provenance and licence record before it is merged.

Feasibility

Target6 weeks, solo, AI-assistedReasoning
10-minute prototypeFeasible, with conditionsA greybox build for playtesters, not a product to sell. It depends on steering passing in week 2, frozen scope, placeholder art and audio, and week 6 kept for playtests.
30-minute full gameNot feasibleEstimated 27–31 weeks solo (about 5 months with stock assets and AI voice). The largest gaps are 3D art and character animation, QA, and Steam launch preparation, including a public Coming Soon page for at least two weeks before release.

AI realistically speeds up code, data, docs and dialogue drafts by 1.3–2×. It helps little with steering feel, visual readability, consistent 3D art and playtesting. The plan therefore commits the six weeks to the prototype, and uses the week-2 steering result and week-6 playtests to decide whether to fund the full game.

Risks

  1. Boat feel is the largest risk. Making waves a stretch goal removes its hardest part from the critical path, and Gate 0 tests it first.
  2. Scope creep across repairs, sequences and environmental detail, held back by the pillars and the scope rule.
  3. Usage limits interrupting work, monitored weekly and handled by routing routine tasks to smaller models.

Appendix B

Beyond scope

Ideas deliberately kept out of the prototype and full game. None is scheduled; each would need to pass the pillars first.

  • Companion choice. Choose Jeff or Daniel before boarding; either can complete both crossings.
  • Daniel as a companion. A flashlight that is faster to read than Jeff’s voice but occasionally wrong. It conflicts with pillar 4, so it would need playtesting first.
  • Hidden coda. An ending unlocked by journal fragments, shrines and symbols, revealing what happened to earlier keepers while leaving the supernatural unconfirmed.
  • Full Gale mode. A New Game+ run that remixes the repairs and crossings into a harder, repeatable shift.
