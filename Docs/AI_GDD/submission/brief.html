Game design submission · condensed brief

Lighting the Storm

An apprentice keeper repairs a failing tower, lets two shipwrecked sailors in from the storm, and crosses a worsening sea for the one part that can’t be fixed on site, before a passenger ship is lost on the rocks.

By Paul25 September 2026First-person · single-player · PC (Steam)Bracken Isle Light · Fl(2) W 15s
Part 1

Executive Summary

You are the new apprentice to Ted, keeper of the lighthouse on Bracken Isle. You repair the building’s failing systems under his direction, but the beacon keeps flickering. A rowboat crashes onto the island; you watch from the window, then open the gate so the survivors, Jeff and Daniel, can get inside. With Jeff, you cross the storm in an engine boat to fetch a replacement burner from an abandoned outpost and race back to fit it before a passenger ship reaches the rocks. The core skill is steering a powered boat past rocks and hazards as the storm worsens. No combat, no monster, no jump scares; folk-horror details stay unexplained.

Design pillars

1 · The light must returnEverything leads back to the beacon: what you repair, what guides you home, what decides the ship’s fate.Test: does it connect to getting the light back?
2 · The storm sets the clockPressure comes from the ship’s lights, weather and visibility, never a timer, meter or enemy.Test: can the player feel time running out without UI?
3 · Your hands, your eyesAll first-person and physical. Guidance comes from Ted and Jeff, not markers.Test: is it happening to the player, or shown to them?
4 · Dread you can readSustained unease, but the player always knows what to do and why they failed.Test: does it add unease without confusion?

Scope rule: one lighthouse, one stretch of sea, one abandoned outpost; no new major locations.

Cast

The Apprentice (player), characterised through action and a handwritten journal. Ted, the gruff keeper who directs repairs out loud. Jeff, a coarse older seaman and the player’s companion on both crossings. Daniel, a younger sailor, shaken and glad to be alive, who stays at the lighthouse.

Part 2

Game Mechanics

One worsening night on duty, played once through the main story.

Core loop

  1. 01
    Repair the lighthouse. Ted names the broken system; the player finds and fixes it. The beacon still flickers.
  2. 05
    Pick up the burner at the outpost.
  3. 02
    Watch the crash, open the gate. Seen from the window; Jeff and Daniel walk in on their own.
  4. 06
    Cross back as the storm peaks: denser hazards, lower visibility.
  5. 03
    Board with Jeff. The hidden ship deadline starts here.
  6. 07
    Fit the burner in the lamp room as the ship closes in.
  7. 04
    Cross to the outpost, steering past rocks and debris to Jeff’s calls.
  8. 08
    Win: the ship sails into port. Fail: it runs aground.

Lighthouse repairs

Interdependent systems (lamp and optic, rotation clockwork, fuel and fog signal, stormproofing) are fixed through short first-person puzzles with a fixed toolkit, not a crafting menu. Ted’s directions are the first assist; their detail scales with difficulty. A handwritten journal tracks what is broken, and some repairs open new areas. When everything else works but the beacon still flickers, Ted goes to the window, and that dead end becomes the inciting incident.

Sea crossing

The engine boat runs at one fixed speed with one control: steering. The challenge is placed hazards (rocks, reefs, buoys, debris) along a route that narrows and darkens as the storm grows; the sea moves for atmosphere only. Jeff’s calls are the second assist and read from the same hazard data as the world, so they never mislead. Aids: a compass, the beam when heading home, and silhouettes in low visibility. A collision takes on water, slows the boat or causes a brief capsize and recovery, all costing time. The burner can be knocked loose on the return but is never lost.

Tension and the deadline

A countdown the player never sees as a number starts on boarding. The ship’s approaching lights and the rising storm show how much time is left. It is the only timer: it runs through both crossings, the pickup and the final repair. Mistakes cost time; only missing the deadline fails the run.

Difficulty, accessibility and presentation

One difficulty preset sets how specific Ted and Jeff are and how intense hazards feel, without changing the story. Calls are subtitled with speaker names; controls are remappable. Everything is first-person, with no cutaways. Warm lamplight plays against blue-grey storm light, the beacon’s beam is the constant visual anchor, and sound is diegetic: wind, rain, machinery, a distant foghorn, no shock stingers.

AI Architecture

One developer works with eight AI agent roles, each owning one department. Each is described by what the player experiences as a result of its work.

AI is used during development only. The shipped game makes no AI calls: dialogue, storm and ship are authored and scripted, so there is no runtime API cost or dependency.

How the agents work

  • Bounded briefs: each task names its goal, inputs, owned files, interfaces and acceptance checks.
  • Human judgement: the developer owns steering feel, tone, art fit and story, and reviews and merges every change.
  • Pillars bind every agent: work that fails a pillar’s test is returned. Unresolved story choices are escalated, never decided silently.
  • Sequence: developer settles decisions → Boat/Physics steering spike (other content waits) → Layout and Systems slice → Lighting and Sound feedback → Dialogue and Asset content → QA and playtests.
  • Handoffs return editable source, licences, test steps and known limits, and must run from a clean checkout.

Godot’s main gap, no built-in ocean, is off the critical path with waves as a stretch goal. Unity is the alternative if the developer knows C#; Unreal shuts out AI help; Phaser can’t meet pillar 3.

Build contract

  • Separately owned scenes (lighthouse, sea, outpost, player) and one game-state controller; content stored as data with stable IDs.
  • Phases: REPAIRS → CRASH_AND_GATE → BOARDING → OUTBOUND → OUTPOST → RETURN → FINAL_REPAIR → WIN | FAIL
  • Invariants: no deadline before boarding; Jeff is the only companion; the outcome resolves exactly once.
  • Audio, UI and dialogue react to events and never change progression. Hazards, boat reaction and Jeff’s calls share one hazard forecast.

API and plan constraints

  • Plan limits: a Claude Team plan with rolling 5-hour and weekly limits and no overage, so hitting a limit pauses work. Prototype pace is estimated at 30–50% of the weekly limit.
  • Caching: exact-prefix matching; stable docs and briefs keep most input as cheap cache reads.
  • Context: 1M tokens on Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5, 200K on Haiku 4.5; narrow briefs keep usage far below this.
  • Opus 5.5: thinking is always on; spend is controlled with the effort setting.
