I’ve figured out a small but polishable start to the game. You start at the lighthouse. The beacon has gone out and a storm is coming. You start at the lighthouse, you must row a boat with an assistant who “knows the waves” to navigate the waters back to shore where a new beacon awaits. The waves are tricky: bigger waves push the boat back, slowing your progress. Smaller waves help you progress. The player can either eyeball it, or get assistance from the assistant on the boat who shines his torch on the next area of the waves to get to. Once you get to shore, You then load the beacon onto the boat, row it back (same mechanics as before), and load the beacon into the lighthouse. If you take too long, a boat crashes into the lighthouse. What’s your opinion both good and bad? Give me a designer and player POV please


This is a **very workable opening**, especially for a first project, because it is small enough to polish while already teaching the player something about Brusskania: the sea is dangerous, the lighthouse matters, and the world does not wait politely for you.

From a **designer POV**, the strongest part is that you have a complete mini-loop rather than just an introductory walk-and-talk:

**Lighthouse → cross dangerous water → retrieve objective → return under pressure → restore lighthouse.**

That is excellent scope discipline. It gives you movement, interaction, an NPC companion, environmental hazards, an objective, feedback, failure conditions, atmosphere, audio and a satisfying visible payoff. You could genuinely build this as a polished vertical slice before committing to the rest of the game.

The wave mechanic also has potential because it isn't simply "avoid obstacles." You're asking the player to **read the environment**. Big waves are unfavourable, smaller waves are favourable, and the assistant effectively acts as an optional navigation aid. That creates an interesting relationship between player skill and NPC help. It also introduces the assistant naturally through gameplay rather than through exposition.

There are, however, a few design risks.

The biggest one is that **rowing there and rowing back could feel repetitive**. Mechanically, the player is doing the same task twice. I'd keep the core mechanic identical but change the circumstances dramatically on the return journey. The storm could have intensified, visibility could have fallen, the beacon could make the boat heavier, the assistant might struggle to identify waves, or the player could now see the endangered ship approaching in the distance. Then the second crossing becomes escalation rather than repetition.

I'd also be careful with how deterministic the waves feel. If a player thinks, "I made the correct choice and the game randomly pushed me backwards anyway," frustration will arrive very quickly. The waves need to be **readable before they hit**. Shape, sound, foam, height, direction and perhaps the movement of the boat itself should all help players understand what is coming.

The assistant's torch is particularly interesting, but I'd avoid making it function like a glowing objective marker saying **GO HERE**. It would be stronger if he were slightly more human:

> "Wait... wait... NOW. Left of that breaker!"

Then his torch sweeps toward a relatively safe patch.

That creates character, tension and uncertainty simultaneously. Later in the game, you could even play with the player's trust in NPC guidance—but for the opening I'd make him reliable so the player learns the system.

The failure condition also needs some care. **"Take too long and a ship crashes"** is dramatically strong, but an invisible countdown timer could feel unfair. Instead, communicate the deadline through the world. Perhaps when you leave the lighthouse you can see lights from an approaching vessel through the rain. On the return journey it is visibly closer. The assistant becomes increasingly concerned. Maybe you hear its foghorn.

That gives you urgency without putting **04:37 REMAINING** in the corner.

From a **player POV**, I think the opening would be immediately understandable. I wake/start in a lighthouse. The light has failed. A storm is approaching. There's a replacement part across the water. Someone needs that beacon restored before something terrible happens.

That's very clean motivation.

I'd also like that my first interaction with the world isn't opening cupboards, reading journals and following an NPC down a corridor. I'm immediately **doing something dangerous**.

The assistant could become a particularly memorable element. Imagine the first crossing: visibility is awful, you're struggling to understand the water, and suddenly his torch sweeps across the waves and he shouts where to go. That's a nice little "oh, I understand this" moment.

My potential frustration as a player would be if rowing felt sluggish. Boats often sound interesting in design documents and then become tedious because the player presses forward while watching scenery crawl past. I'd want fairly responsive controls and relatively short crossings—perhaps **2–4 minutes each**, depending on how eventful they are.

I'd also want to understand why I'm personally doing this. Something as simple as the assistant yelling:

> "The lamp's dead. Replacement's waiting at the old harbour. If that ship reaches the headland before we're lit, it'll never see the rocks."

Now I understand **what, where, why and what happens if I fail** within about ten seconds.

There's another thing I particularly like given the larger Brusskania idea you've been developing: **starting at the lighthouse is thematically clever.** You've been thinking about the lighthouse as a destination—an escape route away from Brusskania. Starting the game there lets the player see this location before they understand its eventual significance.

Much later, if the player finally fights their way back to this same lighthouse intending to escape the country, the place has history.

At the beginning:

**The lighthouse is somewhere you protect.**

At the end:

**The lighthouse is somewhere you escape from.**

That's potentially a very satisfying structural bookend.

One change I'd seriously consider is making the opening disaster **almost succeed rather than simply succeed**. You restore the beacon at the last possible moment. The light sweeps across the storm. Through the rain you briefly see the ship alter course away from the rocks.

Then perhaps something about that ship, its cargo, its markings, its passengers—or why the beacon inexplicably failed—becomes the **first tiny thread of the larger conspiracy**.

Don't explain it yet.

Just give the player one thing that feels slightly wrong.

That would turn what is already a nice tutorial into the beginning of the story.
